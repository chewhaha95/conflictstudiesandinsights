01

Russia–Ukraine

Ground pressure, deep strikes and drone adaptation.

  • Ground operations. Russian forces advanced southeast of Dobropillya and continued infiltrating the town, supporting efforts to outflank Ukraine’s defensive belt from the southwest.
  • Deep strikes. Ukraine struck Russian defence-industrial facilities, including the Proton-Electrotex electronics plant in Oryol and the Energiya battery plant in Lipetsk Oblast, targeting supply chains that support Russian weapons production.
  • Drone warfare and connectivity. Russia’s jet-powered drones continued to challenge Ukrainian air defences. Temporary Starshield restrictions affected high-speed interceptor-drone operations from 14 to 24 October; Ukrainian officials reported access restored by 24 October.

Source: ISW, 24 Oct · Critical Threats, 25 Oct

02

US–Israel–Iran

US force movements amid renewed strike debate. Reports indicated that the US was sending the USS Theodore Roosevelt carrier group and the 13th Marine Expeditionary Unit towards the Middle East, although they were expected to arrive only by late November, and accounts differed on whether the carrier would reinforce or replace existing forces. Israeli media also reported disagreement between Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and IDF Chief of Staff Eyal Zamir over potential military action involving Iran ahead of Israel’s election; Netanyahu denied a confrontation. Meanwhile, the Saudi-led coalition reported intercepting Houthi ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh, Taif and Yanbu, underscoring continued regional escalation risks. Source: Yahoo News · JFeed · Critical Threats, 13 Oct

Other conflicts · Israel–Lebanon, Israel–Gaza, Russia–Europe
03

Israel–Lebanon

US-mediated talks on security arrangements and Israeli withdrawal. During 13–26 October, Israeli and Lebanese delegations held US-mediated military and technical talks at CENTCOM headquarters in Tampa to advance an existing security framework, including pilot-area arrangements, Hezbollah disarmament and gradual Israeli withdrawal. Meanwhile, Israeli artillery strikes and demolitions continued across southern Lebanon, and Hezbollah’s acknowledgement that seized explosives belonged to the group highlighted ongoing friction with Lebanese authorities over weapons control. Source: Times of Israel · Reuters, 15 Oct · Shafaq News

04

Israel–Gaza

Commercial supply disruptions amid continued strikes. Israel temporarily halted commercial goods entering Gaza over a dispute about a reported 10,000-shekel per-truck fee, before announcing their gradual resumption on 19 October. Strikes continued across Gaza, including an incident at the Islamic University in Gaza City on 25 October, where the Israeli army said it had targeted a Hamas military-wing operative and diverted munitions after identifying uninvolved people; the incident is under review. Source: Al Jazeera, 15 Oct · Reuters, 19 Oct · Al Jazeera, 25 Oct

05

Russia–Europe

NATO reinforces Black Sea surveillance amid suspected Russian hybrid activity. Attacks on commercial vessels near Romania and Bulgaria prompted NATO to announce additional maritime patrol aircraft, radar systems, drones and intelligence personnel to improve surveillance and data sharing. Separately, a European public-broadcaster investigation documented physical hybrid incidents that authorities had linked to Russia, and the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre published a resilience-and-deterrence approach combining anticipation, active defence and retaliation; this is a research publication, not an adopted EU defence framework. Attribution of individual maritime attacks remains under investigation. Source: Stars and Stripes, 20 Oct · Al Jazeera, 20 Oct · EBU · EU Joint Research Centre, 23 Oct

What was reportedUkrainian officials reported that Starlink/Starshield restrictions affected connectivity for high-speed platforms, including interceptor drones, and constrained their use against faster Russian threats. Operating speeds were reportedly restored by 24 October. Source: RFE/RL · AP
Operating problemRestrictions cutting connectivity above 160 km/h affected Ukrainian drones that needed satellite communications during high-speed operations, including interception missions.
CountermeasureUkraine pursued removal of the restriction and explored switching to a separate Starshield service package designed for aircraft, without the speed limit. It also examined alternative satellite providers, including OneWeb and Amazon’s developing network. These were reported efforts, not confirmed operational substitutes. Ukrainian officials reported restored operating speeds on 24 October.
What remains unprovenRestoring the existing service addressed the immediate constraint, but does not show that Ukraine has eliminated its underlying dependency.
Preliminary observations

Ukraine pursued both immediate service restoration and longer-term alternatives, rather than relying solely on a technical workaround. The case suggests that service permissions and contractual operating conditions warrant examination alongside a platform’s technical specifications.

What was reportedUkrainian energy officials acknowledged that individual facilities and transformers could be protected, but that extensive exposed infrastructure could not all be shielded. Source: Ukrainska Pravda, 19 Oct
Operating problemRepeated attacks on generation and transmission infrastructure caused power outages, while the scale of the network made comprehensive physical protection impractical. Source: Kyiv Independent · UA.News
CountermeasureUkraine reported combining physical protection with distributed generation, stocks of replacement transformers and equipment, rapid repairs, and backup power for critical facilities such as water pumping stations. Emergency repair work proceeded where safety conditions permitted.
What remains unprovenThe reporting does not quantify how much each measure reduced outages, shortened restoration times or sustained military operations. Statements about stockpiles and repair capacity are principally Ukrainian official accounts.
Preliminary observations

Ukraine’s reported approach combined preventing damage, sustaining essential functions and restoring damaged assets. Replacement equipment and repair capacity were treated as resilience measures, not merely post-attack administrative requirements. Distributed generation was being pursued, but officials also acknowledged that installed capacity remained insufficient in some areas; it should not be presented as a completed solution.

Questions for us

Which essential functions require physical protection, independent backup power and pre-positioned replacement equipment, and how long can they operate before restoration?

QuestionPreliminary findings
Operating problemAttacks on civilian infrastructure and commercial activity can create security pressure before the perpetrator and intent are established, complicating decisions on protection, attribution and escalation.
Specific observationDrone attacks on commercial shipping near Romania and Bulgaria raised concerns about spillover into NATO territory. Separately, a European broadcaster investigation documented physical hybrid incidents with varying levels of attribution to Russia. These should not be treated as one proven, centrally coordinated campaign. Source: EBU
CountermeasureNATO announced additional maritime patrol aircraft, radar systems, drones, intelligence personnel and forensic capabilities on 20 October. The response prioritised situational awareness, information sharing and investigation rather than immediate military retaliation. Source: Stars and Stripes, 20 Oct
What remains unprovenThe available evidence does not establish Russian responsibility for every maritime attack or hybrid incident, show that all incidents formed a coordinated campaign, or show that NATO’s additional capabilities deterred later attacks. The announced deployments should not be presented as proven countermeasures.
Preliminary observations

NATO’s announced response combined surveillance with investigative capabilities, addressing both the immediate need to detect threats and the later need to establish responsibility.

The case suggests that protective action need not wait for definitive attribution, although decisions on retaliation require a separate evidential and political assessment.

The broadcaster investigation distinguished incidents by the source and strength of attribution, a useful way to avoid conflating suspicion, official allegations and confirmed findings.

Question for us

How should units rehearse their response to suspected sabotage or unauthorised drone activity near camps and key installations, including reporting, immediate protection, evidence preservation and escalation through the chain of command?
